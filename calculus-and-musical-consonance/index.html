The Applications of the Calculus to the Mathematics of Musical Consonance

A monograph in mathematical acoustics by Alec R. Stevens, exploring the calculus behind consonance, dissonance, harmonics, and Fourier analysis.

Monograph in Mathematical Acoustics May 2021 Alec R. Stevens
Open PDF Back to Home

Overview

This thesis develops a mathematically rigorous account of the relationship between calculus and the perception of musical consonance and dissonance. It treats equal temperament, sinusoidal pressure, harmonic structure, and Fourier decomposition as the main analytic tools.

Abstract

The present treatise develops a mathematically rigorous account of the relationship between the calculus and the perception of musical consonance and dissonance. The investigation proceeds from a single governing question: by what mathematical structures may the perceived ordering of musical intervals from consonant to dissonant be characterized, and to what extent does that ordering admit principled derivation from the apparatus of differential and integral calculus? The analysis is organized around four analytical instruments: the geometric sequence underlying twelve-tone equal temperament, the definite integral of the sinusoidal pressure waveform, the divergent series of integer-multiple harmonics that constitutes the spectrum of an idealized vibrating string, and the Fourier decomposition that connects time-domain waveforms to their spectral content.

The treatment begins with a corrected formulation of the equal-tempered frequency relation, which is shown to be a geometric sequence rather than a geometric series, and traces the consequences of that distinction for the analytic description of pitch. It then examines the integral of one positive half-cycle of the sinusoidal pressure waveform, demonstrating that the ratio of such integrals between octave-related notes recovers, but does not exceed, the ordinary inverse relationship between frequency and wavelength. The harmonic series of overtones is then introduced, and its mathematical properties are examined as they bear upon the perceived sonority of a single note. Fourier analysis is then introduced as the canonical framework within which timbre is decomposed and within which the heuristic observations of music theory are placed into a quantitative setting.
